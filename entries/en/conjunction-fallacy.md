## How it works

Two combined events can never be more probable than either one alone. Yet, in Tversky and Kahneman's Linda problem (1983), a woman is described as engaged in social causes. A majority of participants then judge it more probable that she is "a bank teller and an activist" than simply "a bank teller".

A more detailed scenario can seem more coherent, and this coherence is mistaken for probability.

Researchers do not agree on the interpretation. Hertwig and Gigerenzer (1999) argue that participants often understand the word "probable" in an everyday rather than mathematical sense, and that the error drops markedly when the question is framed in terms of frequencies.

## Why it is problematic

Each detail added to a story makes the whole less probable, while making it seem more credible. A very precise scenario chaining several steps can thus persuade more than a simpler hypothesis, precisely because it tells a complete story.

## How to detect it

- A scenario piles up details and steps that all have to be true.
- Its precision is presented as a sign of its soundness.
- A forecast combines several conditions without stating the probability of each.

## How to protect yourself

- Break the scenario into steps and ask whether each one is established.
- Remember that the probability of a chain falls with each added condition.
- Reformulate in frequencies: "Out of 100 comparable cases, how many combine all these elements?"
