## How it works

Two logically equivalent wordings do not produce the same judgment. Options presented as gains favor preferring certainty; the same options presented as losses favor preferring risk.

In Tversky and Kahneman's (1981) "Asian disease" problem, participants choose between a program that saves 200 people out of 600 for certain and a program that offers a one-in-three chance of saving everyone. Framed in lives saved, the majority choose the sure program; framed in deaths (400 people out of 600 will die for certain), the majority choose the risky program, although the consequences are identical.

## Why it is problematic

The choice depends on the choice of words, not on the facts. Whoever controls the wording steers the decision without changing a single figure.

## How to detect it

- The same result is presented sometimes as a success rate, sometimes as a failure rate, depending on what serves the message.
- Only the gains or only the losses of an option are mentioned.
- The reference point (the "normal" state being compared against) is not made explicit.

## How to protect yourself

- Reword the information yourself in the other frame (gains turned into losses, and vice versa) and check whether the judgment changes.
- Ask for the raw figures, with the denominator.
- Ask what reference point was chosen, and why.
