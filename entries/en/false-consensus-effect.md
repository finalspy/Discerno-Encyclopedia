## How it works

Ross, Greene and House (1977) asked students whether they would agree to walk around campus wearing an advertising sandwich board. Those who agreed estimated that most others would also agree, and those who refused estimated that most would refuse.

Marks and Miller (1987) review a decade of work and discuss several explanations: we tend to associate with people who resemble us, our own ideas come more readily to mind, and we need to feel that they are shared.

## Why it is problematic

Believing oneself to be in the majority can lead one to see a disagreement as an anomaly or as bad faith. News feeds and homogeneous circles can reinforce this impression. A statement such as "everybody knows that" can give the appearance of a consensus, without any measurement.

## How to detect it

- Phrases such as "everyone thinks that", "people are fed up", "nobody believes anymore that", with no figures.
- An opinion is presented as obvious common sense, and its opponents as a negligible minority.
- A few online reactions are presented as the general view.

## How to protect yourself

- Look for a survey whose method is published, before talking about what "people" think.
- Ask how your own circle and news feeds differ from the population as a whole.
- Estimate a figure before looking it up, then compare.
