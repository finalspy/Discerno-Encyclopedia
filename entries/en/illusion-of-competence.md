## How it works

What is in front of us looks easy: rereading a lesson, following a worked solution or getting an answer from a tool gives a feeling of ease that we take for mastery. Koriat and Bjork (2005) show that students overestimate what they will remember when the answer is present during study, although it will be absent at test time. Bjork, Dunlosky and Kornell (2013) review these judgments: rereading and ease of reading give a feeling of knowing greater than actual knowledge.

Fisher, Goddu and Keil (2015) observe a related effect with an outside source: after searching the Internet for explanations, participants rate themselves as better able to explain topics, including topics unrelated to their search, as if the knowledge reachable through the tool had become their own.

Neutral example: after following a recipe step by step in a video, one feels able to make it from memory, and forgets half of the steps.

## Why it is problematic

People stop learning too early, take on tasks they cannot carry out alone and miss the errors in a result they could not have produced. The gap shows when the help is gone: an exam, a breakdown, an unexpected case.

## How to detect it

- The feeling of mastery comes from having read, watched or obtained, not from having redone it without help.
- One recognizes the right answer but can neither produce nor explain it.
- A result obtained with a tool or a tutorial is taken as proof of one's own competence.

## How to protect yourself

- Test yourself without support: redo, explain or recall from memory.
- Ask: "Could I redo this without the help, and spot an error in it?"
- Explain the reasoning to someone else, or in writing.
