## How it works

When the quality of a piece of work is hard to assess, people fall back on the perceived effort. Kruger, Wirtz, Van Boven and Altermatt (2004) showed the same poems, paintings or suits of armor to participants: those told that the work had taken more time judged it better and valued it more, especially when they had trouble judging its quality directly. Morales (2005) observes that customers reward a firm more when they see its effort, for the same result.

The same mechanism works the other way: work whose effort cannot be seen, because it rests on accumulated experience or looks instant, is underestimated. The complexity an expert masters becomes invisible to someone who only sees how fast the result came.

Neutral example: a craftsman fixes in five minutes a fault that others had not found, and the customer finds the price excessive for "so little work".

## Why it is problematic

People admire and pay for what looks laborious, and underestimate the expertise that makes a task quick: a few minutes of a specialist's work can rest on years of practice. Putting effort forward can also be used to pass off mediocre work.

## How to detect it

- The value of a result is judged by the time spent or the visible toil, not by its quality.
- A task done quickly is judged easy, and therefore within anyone's reach.
- A presentation dwells on the hours spent rather than on what the result brings.

## How to protect yourself

- Judge a result against quality criteria set in advance.
- Ask: "What did one need to know for this to be quick?"
- Keep the time spent apart from the value obtained.
