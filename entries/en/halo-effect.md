## How it works

A salient trait, such as appearance, likability or success, colors the evaluation of other traits. Thorndike (1920) noticed this in the ratings that officers gave their subordinates: distinct qualities (bearing, intelligence, loyalty) were rated in a highly correlated way.

Nisbett and Wilson (1977) had two groups rate the same teacher, presented either as warm or as distant. The group exposed to the warm version also rated his appearance and accent more favorably, and did not recognize this influence.

## Why it is problematic

The credibility of a message is judged on the charm, fame or success of the person delivering it, rather than on its arguments. The effect works in both directions: a negative impression discredits statements that are otherwise accurate.

## How to detect it

- A claim is backed by the person's fame, looks or success, with no link to the subject.
- A person recognized in one field is presented as competent in another.
- One flaw of the person is used to dismiss everything they say.

## How to protect yourself

- Assess each claim separately from the person who makes it.
- Ask yourself: "Would I say the same thing if it came from someone else?"
- Look for sources and method, not reputation.
