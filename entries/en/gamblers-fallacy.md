## How it works

After several "heads", many people expect "tails", as if chance had to make up for it. A fair coin, however, has no memory: the probability stays one in two on every toss.

Tversky and Kahneman (1971, 1974) link this error to the belief that a small sample must already resemble the whole: one expects a short series to look balanced, which chance does not guarantee.

The reasoning applies only to independent events. If the outcome depends on the previous one, as with cards drawn without replacement, the probability does change.

## Why it is problematic

This belief can push people to keep betting in order to "win back" their losses, and to read a run of chance as a promise. It is also used as a sales argument when a game or an investment is presented as being "due".

## How to detect it

- Phrases such as "it cannot keep going like this", "it is bound to turn", or "its turn is coming soon", without an explanation of the mechanism.
- A forecast relies only on the time elapsed since the last event.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask whether the events are really linked to one another, and by what mechanism.
- Reason about compensation only for very large numbers: proportions converge toward the average, without any "catching up" on the past.
- Set a loss limit in advance, rather than deciding during a streak.
