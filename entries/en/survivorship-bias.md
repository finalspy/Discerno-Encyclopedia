## How it works

A sample made up only of what survived a selection is not representative of the original group. The eliminated cases are no longer visible and therefore do not weigh on the analysis.

The classic example is that of the statistician Abraham Wald during the Second World War (Wald, 1943; Mangel and Samaniego, 1984). Planes that returned from missions bore impacts concentrated in certain places. Wald pointed out that the places with no impacts on those planes were the ones that needed reinforcing: planes hit in those places did not return.

## Why it is problematic

Success stories, "secrets of successful people" or methods that "have proven themselves" ignore those who did the same thing without succeeding. The observed correlation may come solely from the selection.

## How to detect it

- The examples cited are all successes (companies, people, methods), and no failure is mentioned.
- A practice is presented as the cause of a success, without knowing how many people used it without success.
- The data come from an already filtered population (current customers, participants who stayed until the end).

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "What became of those who did the same thing without succeeding?"
- Look for the denominator: how many cases at the start, how many at the end?
- Be wary of conclusions drawn from a sample that has already been through a selection.
