## How it works

Benesch (2012) proposes five criteria for judging whether a statement is "dangerous", that is, likely to help make violence acceptable: the influence of the speaker, the receptiveness of the audience, the content (for example dehumanization), the historical and social context, and the means of dissemination. The Rabat Plan of Action (UN, 2012) retains six factors for distinguishing punishable incitement from a shocking but protected statement: context, speaker, intent, content, scope, and the likelihood that violence will follow.

A call to violence is not always explicit: it comes through phrases ("we should put an end to them"), veiled threats, the designation of targets, or congratulations addressed to past violence.

## Why it is problematic

It endangers real people and can turn tensions into violence. It makes violence seem ordinary, and therefore more likely.

## How to detect it

- Statements that wish for, encourage or excuse violence against a group.
- The designation of identifiable targets, sometimes with their personal information.
- Formulas that present violence as self-defense or as a duty.

## How to protect yourself

- Apply Benesch's criteria: who is speaking, to whom, in what context, with what words?
- Distinguish violent speech from shocking or provocative speech that calls for no act.
- Avoid passing on calls to violence, including to denounce them.

## How to respond

- Report the content to the platform and, if it constitutes a threat or illegal incitement, to the competent authorities.
- Keep a record (dated screenshot) to support the report.
- Support the people targeted, and warn those who are threatened.
