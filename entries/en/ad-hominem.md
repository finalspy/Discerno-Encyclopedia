## How it works

The reply targets the author of an argument (their character, motives, past or affiliation) rather than the argument itself, and concludes that the argument is false or worthless.

Walton (1998) distinguishes several forms: the direct attack, questioning the person's interests, and the *tu quoque* ("you too"). He also notes that questioning a person's reliability is not always fallacious.

Neutral example: "His figures are wrong because he is an unpleasant person."

## Why it is problematic

Whether an argument is true does not depend on the person who puts it forward. Attacking the person turns the discussion away from the substance and can lead to accurate statements being dismissed.

## How to detect it

- The reply is about the person (character, private life, affiliation), not about what they said.
- No objection to the content is raised.
- The conclusion "what he says is false" rests on a flaw of the person.

Do not confuse: pointing out a conflict of interest or a lack of qualification can be relevant to weigh a testimony or an expert opinion, as long as the argument itself is still examined.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "Does the argument hold, regardless of who says it?"
- Answer the content before discussing the person.
- Separate the reliability of a witness from the validity of a reasoning you can check yourself.
