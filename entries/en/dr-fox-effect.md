## How it works

In the experiment by Naftulin, Ware and Donnelly (1973), an actor presented as an expert gave a lecture in a warm, expressive style, on content deliberately devoid of meaning. Before an audience of professionals, most found the talk stimulating and clear.

The study has methodological limits (small sample, no control group) and has been debated. It nonetheless illustrates a result confirmed later: in a synthesis of several studies, a teacher's expressiveness clearly influences students' ratings, and much less their measured learning (Abrami, Leventhal and Perry, 1982).

## Why it is problematic

Confidence, charisma and fluency are taken as evidence of competence. Weak or false content can seem convincing, and accurate content poorly presented can be dismissed.

## How to detect it

- The talk is brilliant but, once summarized, one no longer knows precisely what it claims.
- The argument rests on ease, humor or vocabulary rather than on evidence.
- Precise questions receive fluent but evasive answers.

## How to protect yourself

- Summarize the claims in writing in two or three sentences, without the style.
- Ask what evidence was given, and which of it would be verifiable.
- Separate the assessment of form (pleasant, clear) from that of substance (accurate, sourced).
