## How it works

Kahneman and Tversky (1973) present descriptions of people drawn from a group made up of engineers and lawyers. Whether the group is 30% or 70% engineers changes the answers little: participants rely on how closely the description resembles the stereotype of the profession.

A worked example, invented to illustrate the calculation: a disease affects 1 person in 1,000, and a test detects it almost always, with a 1% false-positive rate. Out of 1,000 people tested, there are about 1 true positive and 10 false positives. A positive test therefore corresponds to a probability of being ill of about 9%, not 99%.

## Why it is problematic

An indicator can seem very reliable while producing mostly false alarms when the phenomenon sought is rare. A figure presented without the base rate can then make a threat, risk or piece of evidence look much stronger than it is.

## How to detect it

- A success rate or percentage is given without the frequency of the phenomenon.
- A vivid portrait or story is used to conclude that a case is probable.
- A comparison is made in absolute numbers between groups of very different sizes.

## How to protect yourself

- Always ask: "Out of how many?" and "What is the usual frequency?"
- Redo the calculation in counts out of 1,000 or 10,000 people, which are easier to read than percentages.
- Distinguish the probability of having the indicator when one has the case from the probability of having the case when one has the indicator.
