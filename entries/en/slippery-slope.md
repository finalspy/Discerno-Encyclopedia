## How it works

It is argued that an action or decision, acceptable in itself, will mechanically set off a chain of events leading to an extreme and undesirable outcome. The conclusion is presented as inevitable, although the intermediate steps are neither established nor justified.

Walton (1992) shows that a slippery slope argument is not always fallacious: it is when the causal chain is not supported, that is, when each step is not made plausible by reasons or data.

Neutral example: "If you take a cookie before dinner, soon you will stop eating real meals."

## Why it is problematic

A moderate measure is rejected because of an extreme consequence that nothing establishes. Fear of a distant outcome replaces the examination of the measure proposed.

## How to detect it

- A chain of "then", "so", "soon" leads from the measure to a catastrophe.
- Each step is asserted; none is argued or quantified.
- The final outcome is far more serious than the initial measure.

## How to protect yourself

- Examine each link in the chain: is it probable, and why?
- Ask which mechanisms or data justify moving from one step to the next.
- Check whether there is a natural stopping point (a rule, a law, a cost) that would prevent the sequence from continuing.
