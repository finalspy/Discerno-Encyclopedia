## How it works

Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory (1979) evaluates outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, rather than as absolute states. It proposes that losses weigh more than equivalent gains: many people refuse a bet with even odds where the possible gain does not clearly exceed the possible loss. Tversky and Kahneman (1992) estimate this ratio at roughly two to one in their data.

The scope of the effect is debated. Gal and Rucker (2018) argue that loss aversion is not a general principle and that other mechanisms explain part of the results. Its proponents still regard it as a robust finding: the debate remains open.

## Why it is problematic

Presenting a choice as a loss to avoid can weigh more than presenting it as a gain to obtain, without the facts changing. This lever appears in offers presented as "not to be missed" and in messages about what one stands to lose.

## How to detect it

- A message insists on what you will lose rather than on what you can obtain.
- Phrases such as "last chance" or "don't let this pass" come with urgency.
- The reference point is chosen so that the proposed option appears to be the only way to avoid a loss.

## How to protect yourself

- Reframe the choice in terms of gains, then compare the two formulations.
- Look at the final outcome rather than the gap from the proposed reference point.
- Ask whether you would choose the same option starting from zero.
