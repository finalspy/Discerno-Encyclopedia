## How it works

Kruger and Dunning (1999) asked participants to take tests (logical reasoning, grammar, humor) and then to estimate their own performance. Participants in the lowest-scoring quartile clearly overestimated their results. The authors explain this by a double handicap: the skills needed to perform well are also those needed to judge the quality of one's own performance.

This reading is debated: part of the observed effect may come from statistical artifacts (Nuhfer et al., 2016; Gignac and Zajenkowski, 2020), and the popularization of the effect ("the incompetent think they are experts") goes beyond what the data establish.

## Why it is problematic

High confidence does not indicate high competence. In a debate, a firm tone can be taken as a sign of expertise. Conversely, the label "Dunning-Kruger" is often used as an insult against an opponent, which is not an argument.

## How to detect it

- A categorical claim on a technical subject is made without sources, without nuance, and without mention of what is not known.
- The person mentions no limits to their reasoning and no known objections.
- For misuse: the term is used to disqualify someone instead of answering their argument.

## How to protect yourself

- Judge a claim by its sources and method, not by the confidence of the person stating it.
- Ask how one could test one's own competence on the subject (feedback from a third party, a test, verification).
- Do not use the term to disqualify a person: discuss the argument.
