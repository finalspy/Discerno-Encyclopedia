## How it works

Seeing others adopt an opinion, a product or a behavior increases the likelihood of adopting it in turn. Leibenstein (1950) describes how demand for a good can rise simply because others consume it.

In Asch's experiments (1956), participants had to compare lines whose answer was obvious. When a unanimous group gave a wrong answer, a substantial share of responses conformed to the group's view.

## Why it is problematic

An idea's popularity is not proof that it is correct, and it can be manufactured: fake reviews, bots, prominently displayed counters. It pushes people to follow a trend without examining the reasons.

## How to detect it

- An argument rests on "everybody does it" or "millions of people have already…".
- Counters (shares, likes, sales) are put forward as proof of quality or truth.
- The impression of consensus is created by the repetition of the same message by several accounts.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask what the number actually proves: interest, quality, or simply reach?
- Look for independent assessments of popularity (studies, trials, reasoned reviews).
- Remember that one can be the only person in a group to be right, or to be wrong.
