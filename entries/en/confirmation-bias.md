## How it works

Faced with a hypothesis, we spontaneously look for cases that confirm it rather than cases that could refute it. We also welcome favorable evidence more readily and scrutinize unfavorable evidence more harshly. Wason (1960) demonstrated this with a simple task: participants almost exclusively tested examples that fit their supposed rule, and drew excessive confidence from them.

Nickerson (1998) describes many forms: selective search for evidence, biased interpretation of ambiguous information, selective memory.

## Why it is problematic

It sustains false beliefs because contrary evidence is never sought or weighed. In a piece of content, it leads to presenting only one side of the question, which gives an impression of solidity that the facts do not justify.

## How to detect it

- All the sources cited point in the same direction, with no mention of a known objection.
- Favorable facts are detailed, while unfavorable facts are absent or dismissed in a sentence.
- The same information is judged reliable when it suits and doubtful when it does not.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "What would make me change my mind?", then actually look for it.
- Read at least one serious source that argues the opposite position.
- Apply the same reliability criteria to sources that suit us and to those that do not.
