## Definition

Arendt (1951) describes totalitarianism as a new form of government, founded on ideology and terror, which aims at the total domination of individuals and not merely at their obedience. Friedrich and Brzezinski (1956) draw up a "syndrome" of six traits: an official ideology covering every aspect of life, a single mass party, a terrorist police, a monopoly of communication, a monopoly of weapons, and a centrally directed economy.

## Competing definitions and debates

The concept was shaped during the Cold War and remains debated. Gleason (1995) traces its history and its role in political debate. Linz (2000) distinguishes totalitarian regimes, characterized by an elaborate ideology and mass mobilization, from authoritarian regimes, which are more limited. The comparison of Nazism and Stalinism, often made under this label, is itself debated (Fitzpatrick and Geyer, 2009): some see a useful comparison, others a risk of erasing differences.

The term describes entire regimes, with their state apparatus. It does not apply to an isolated speech or to a single person.

## Why it helps to discern

The concept shows that the mechanisms that characterize totalitarian power (monopoly of information, terror, compulsory ideology, mass mobilization) do not depend on the political label of the ideology: the regimes historically grouped under it belonged to opposing camps. It helps identify, in a discourse, the ambition for total control rather than the content of the ideas.
