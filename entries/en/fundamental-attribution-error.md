## How it works

In Jones and Harris's (1967) experiment, participants read a text that defends a political position. Even when they know that the author was instructed to defend that position, they tend to think it reflects the author's real opinion.

Ross (1977) gave this phenomenon its name: one overestimates a person's dispositions and underestimates the constraints of their situation. Gilbert and Malone (1995) analyze the conditions under which it appears.

Its generality is debated. Miller (1984) observes differences between cultures in how behavior is explained, which is a reason not to present it as universal and uniform.

## Why it is problematic

An error, a delay or a stance can be presented as proof of a character or a bad intention, without examining the context. The debate then slides from facts to people.

## How to detect it

- An isolated act is used to describe what someone "is".
- A hidden intention is asserted without anything establishing it.
- The text says nothing about the constraints, the role, or the information available to the person.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask what an ordinary person would have done in the same situation.
- Look for at least one explanation linked to the context before concluding on character.
- Distinguish what was done, which can be observed, from the motives, which are inferred.
