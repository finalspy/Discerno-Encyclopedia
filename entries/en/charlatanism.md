## Definition

The charlatan claims to hold knowledge, a skill or an effective product that he or she does not have, and gains an advantage from it (money, fame, authority). The word comes from the Italian *ciarlatano*, the traveling seller who harangues the crowd.

Its methods overlap with those of other entries: vague statements that apply to everyone (Barnum effect), confidence and style replacing content (Dr Fox effect), hollow language that sounds profound, testimonials in place of evidence, urgency and scarcity (Cialdini, 1984).

## Competing definitions and debates

The notion presupposes an intention to deceive, which is hard to establish: a person sincerely convinced of an ineffective remedy is not a charlatan in the strict sense, even if the effect on the public is the same. The law deals mainly with effects (misleading commercial practices, unlawful practice of a regulated profession) and differs from one country to another.

A practice without evidence of effectiveness can also bring real well-being (placebo effect, social support). This does not make it proof of efficacy, but it explains its success.

## Why it helps to discern

Rather than investigating intentions, look at what can be verified: what evidence, what references, what measured results, what answer to precise questions? A useful starting doubt: would the pitch be the same for any client, and can the promise be tested?
