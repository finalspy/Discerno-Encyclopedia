## How it works

Small and Loewenstein (2003) observe that participants give more when the beneficiary is already designated than when it remains to be determined, even without other information about them.

Small, Loewenstein and Slovic (2007) compare donation appeals based on the portrait of a girl and on statistics. The portrait elicits more donations, and adding statistics to the portrait does not increase them.

## Why it is problematic

The reaction does not follow the size of the problem. A single well-chosen story can direct attention, emotion and decisions, while saying nothing about the number of people concerned or whether the case is representative.

An individual story is not misleading in itself: it can be true and make a fact understandable. The question is whether it illustrates a quantified reality or replaces it.

## How to detect it

- A single, highly personal case is used as an argument for a general conclusion.
- No order of magnitude accompanies the story.
- The text calls for an immediate reaction based on this single story alone.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask how many people are concerned, and whether this case is representative.
- Ask what the reaction would have been to the same situation presented in figures.
- Acknowledge the emotion, then check the data before donating, sharing or deciding.
