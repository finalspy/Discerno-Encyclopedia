## How it works

Johnson and Seifert (1994) had participants read an account of a fire in which an initial piece of information suggested a possible cause, and then that information was withdrawn. Participants remembered the correction, but went on relying on the initial information to explain the facts.

Lewandowsky and colleagues (2012) propose an explanation: the false information becomes part of the mental account, and removing it without replacing it leaves a gap.

## Why it is problematic

Publishing a false claim and correcting it later does not wipe the slate clean. The initial impression can keep steering judgments, especially if the correction is less visible than the original claim.

## How to detect it

- A withdrawn claim comes back in the form: "it was still said that".
- The correction is published with far less visibility than the initial information.
- An insinuation is made as a question, then withdrawn with no visible consequence.

## How to protect yourself

- When correcting, also give the accurate explanation that replaces the error.
- Put the accurate fact forward, rather than repeating the error in a headline.
- Before citing a striking piece of information, check whether it has been corrected since.
- Revisit your conclusions when a source is withdrawn, and not only the source itself.
