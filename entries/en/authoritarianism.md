## Definition

In the political sense, an authoritarian regime is characterized, according to Linz (2000), by limited pluralism, the absence of an elaborate ideology, little mass mobilization, and power exercised by a leader or a small group within ill-defined limits.

In the psychological sense, authoritarianism is an individual disposition. Adorno and his colleagues (1950) described it in relation to the "authoritarian personality". Altemeyer (1981) measures it through submission to established authorities, aggression towards those who depart from the norm, and conformism. Stenner (2005) argues that it is a predisposition that is activated above all when a threat to the common order is perceived.

## Competing definitions and debates

Altemeyer's scales are built around established authority and conventional values, which has led to debate about the existence of left-wing authoritarianism. Conway and his colleagues (2018) argue that it exists and measure it; Costello and his colleagues (2022) propose a dedicated scale (aggression towards the dominant, censorship from above, anticonformism). The quality of these measures, their equivalence with classic authoritarianism and their relative weight across contexts remain disputed.

The two meanings of the word do not overlap: an authoritarian person can live in a free regime, and an authoritarian regime can contain many people who are not authoritarian.

## Why it helps to discern

The concept shifts the question from the *content* of ideas to the *manner* of defending them: submission to an authority, intolerance of disagreement, hostility towards those who step outside the frame. These marks can be found in opposing camps. They are recognized through observable behavior, not through the label a group gives itself.
