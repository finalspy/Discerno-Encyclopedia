## How it works

To estimate how often a phenomenon occurs, the mind uses a shortcut: the easier it is to recall examples, the more common the phenomenon seems. Ease of recall also depends on factors unrelated to actual frequency: dramatic character, recency, media coverage, personal experience.

Tversky and Kahneman (1973) showed that, given a list in which one of the two sexes is represented by more famous names, participants judge that sex to be more numerous in the list. Lichtenstein and her colleagues (1978) observed that dramatic or heavily publicized causes of death are overestimated, and mundane causes underestimated.

## Why it is problematic

Risks and trends are judged by what stands out, not by what is measured. Content that multiplies striking examples gives the impression of a high frequency without any figure establishing it.

## How to detect it

- A claim about how frequent a phenomenon is rests on a series of anecdotes or recent cases, without a rate or denominator.
- A rare but striking event is presented as revealing a general trend.
- The examples cited are all recent or all heavily publicized.

## How to protect yourself

- Look for the statistic: how many cases out of how many comparable situations, over what period?
- Ask whether the examples that come to mind are representative or merely striking.
- Compare with a data source independent of the media narrative.
