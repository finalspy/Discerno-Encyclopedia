## Definition

Affective polarization is the tendency to judge members of the opposing side negatively and those of one's own side positively (Iyengar et al., 2012). It is measured with scales of warmth, trust or social distance. It differs from ideological polarization, which is the gap between the positions held on specific issues: two groups can hold similar ideas and loathe each other, or the reverse.

## Competing definitions and debates

The idea that societies are increasingly divided is an empirical claim, and the findings vary. Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro (2024) compare several countries: affective polarization rose sharply in some (including the United States) and fell in others. The role of social media, news media and political institutions in these trends is debated.

Perception matters too: people often overestimate how extreme those who think differently are (Ahler and Sood, 2018), and an American study describes a large "exhausted" majority, tired of the conflicts and little visible in public debate (Hawkins et al., 2018). These findings concern specific contexts and do not generalize without caution.

## Why it helps to discern

The concept invites us to tell three things apart: what people think, what they feel toward the other side, and the picture we are given of that other side. A discourse that offers only two sides, or presents every disagreement as hostility, feeds polarization, whichever side it comes from. Asking "do the people on the other side really think this, for the most part?" is a first reflex.
