## How it works

Dahl (1971) makes public contestation a criterion of democracy: the right to oppose, to criticize and to speak freely. Levitsky and Ziblatt (2018) count among the signs of authoritarianism the readiness to restrict the freedoms of opponents, including those of the press. Friedrich and Brzezinski (1956) list the monopoly of communication and a terrorist police among the traits of totalitarian regimes.

Suppression takes varied forms, from the mildest to the most severe: social pressure and ostracism, harassment, pressure on employers or funders, control of media and platforms, legal restrictions, imprisonment.

## Why it is problematic

Without free criticism, errors are not corrected and abuses are not exposed. Public expression is replaced by self-censorship.

## How to detect it

- Critics are silenced through threats, collective harassment or calls for them to be fired.
- Independent media, researchers or judges are targeted as such.
- Expressing disagreement becomes risky, socially or materially.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask whether you are censoring yourself out of fear of the consequences, and for what reasons.
- Distinguish sharp criticism of an idea, which remains discussion, from pressure exerted on a person to make them be quiet.
- Apply the swap test: would you accept the same pressure against your own positions?

## How to respond

- Defend the right to speak, even for opinions you disapprove of, by responding with arguments.
- Publicly or discreetly support the people targeted, and document the facts.
- Use legal avenues: appeals, reports, associations defending civil liberties.
