## How it works

Nyhan and Reifler (2010) gave participants fake newspaper articles containing a claim, followed by a correction. In some cases, the correction strengthened the false belief among the people whose convictions it contradicted.

This result has been widely cited, but later work calls for caution. On many subjects, Wood and Porter (2019) rarely observe this effect: on average, participants move closer to the corrected information. Swire-Thompson, DeGutis and Lazer (2020) show that measurement problems can also make such an effect appear.

The current state of knowledge therefore does not allow it to be presented as a general reaction.

## Why it is problematic

Two errors are possible. The first is to expect that a correction will be enough to change an opinion, when the belief may persist for other reasons. The second is to give up on correcting for fear of making things worse, when most recent results do not support that fear.

## How to detect it

- Someone claims that "there is no point correcting, it just entrenches people in their ideas", without a recent source.
- A hostile reaction to a correction is taken as proof of a change of belief.
- A single study is presented as a general law.

## How to protect yourself

- Correct anyway, with precise, sourced facts and without targeting the person.
- Distinguish what someone says in reaction from what they believe afterwards.
- Check whether a famous result has been replicated before citing it.
