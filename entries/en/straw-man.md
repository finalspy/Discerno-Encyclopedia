## How it works

The opponent's argument is replaced with a weaker, more extreme or simplified version, and that version is then refuted. The reasoning that follows may be valid, but it addresses a position that no one actually defended.

Neutral example: "I'd like us to turn the music down a bit, it's late." Reply: "So you want us to spend the evening in complete silence?"

## Why it is problematic

The rebuttal seems successful although the actual argument was never examined. The audience leaves with a false idea of the position being criticized, and the debate turns to the wrong subject.

## How to detect it

- The position attributed to the other side is introduced with "so you're saying that...", "basically, he wants..." or "those who think this...".
- It is more extreme or more absolute than the terms the person used.
- No exact quotation of what the person said accompanies the rebuttal.

## How to protect yourself

- Compare the refuted position with the exact quotation from the source.
- Restate the other person's position yourself in its strongest version, and ask them to confirm it before you respond.
- Answer the question "Who actually said that?" before judging the rebuttal.
