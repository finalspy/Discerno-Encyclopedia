## Definition

Obscurantism refers to opposition to the spread of knowledge and to critical examination: access to information is hindered, questions are discouraged or banned, and ignorance or submission to an authority is valued over verification.

The word comes from the *Epistolae obscurorum virorum* ("Letters of Obscure Men", 1515-1517), a satire by humanists against opponents of the scholar Reuchlin. It took on its modern meaning with the Enlightenment: Kant (1784) defined the Enlightenment as the exit from a state of minority for which one is oneself responsible, through the use of one's own reason.

## Competing definitions and debates

The term is polemical: whoever uses it places themselves on the side of the Enlightenment, and the label is often used to discredit. It has been applied to religious institutions, political powers, commercial interests (secrecy over data) and ideologies of every orientation.

Believing is not being obscurantist: a personal conviction, religious or not, differs from obstructing others' access to knowledge or forbidding the examination of an empirical question. Anti-intellectualism (Hofstadter, 1963) is close but distinct: it refers to distrust of intellectuals and expertise.

The critique of the Enlightenment itself (Horkheimer and Adorno, 1947) reminds us that reason can also become an instrument of domination, which argues against treating the label as a final judgment.

## Why it helps to discern

The concept shifts the question: it is not about knowing *what* a person believes, but whether one can examine, question and verify. A few markers applicable to any side: is access to sources possible? Is criticism allowed without sanction? Are ignorance or age presented as virtues that exempt one from providing proof?
