## Definition

Rokeach (1960) describes dogmatism as a way of organizing beliefs into a closed system: beliefs are isolated from one another, trust rests on absolute authority, and those who think differently are rejected or devalued. What defines dogmatism is the *structure* of thinking, not its content: one can be dogmatic on any subject.

## Competing definitions and debates

Holding a conviction firmly is not dogmatism: a person can be very sure of their position and remain open to evidence. Dogmatism is recognized by the fact that no evidence could change one's mind.

The link with political orientation is debated. Jost and colleagues (2003) argue that certain measures of closed-mindedness are higher on the right. Ditto and colleagues (2019), in a meta-analysis, find a partisan bias of comparable magnitude on the left and on the right. The two readings rest on different measures and samples, and the discussion is not settled.

## Why it helps to discern

A simple question applies to any discourse, including one's own: "What would make me change my mind?" The absence of an answer, the idea that disagreement is a moral fault, or the idea that only members of the group can understand, are signs of closed-mindedness, whatever the subject defended.
