## How it works

When the outcome of an event is known, we reconstruct its past so that it fits that outcome: we overestimate the probability we would have assigned to it in advance, and we forget the doubts we then had.

Fischhoff (1975) presented participants with a description of an event, with or without its outcome. Those who knew the outcome assigned it a markedly higher probability than those who did not, and did not gauge the influence of that information on their judgment.

## Why it is problematic

Past decisions that turned out badly are judged harshly and draw false lessons, since one believes the outcome was obvious. "We should have seen it coming" accounts ignore the information that was actually available at the time.

## How to detect it

- A narrative presents the warning signs of an event as obvious, without showing what the actors knew at the time.
- Old forecasts are cited selectively, only those that came true.
- The known outcome is used to judge the quality of a decision, regardless of the uncertainty at the time.

## How to protect yourself

- Write down your forecasts and your reasons before knowing the outcome.
- Ask what information was available at the time, and what was not.
- Distinguish the quality of a decision from the quality of its outcome.
