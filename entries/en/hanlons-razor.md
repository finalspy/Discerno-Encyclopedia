## Definition

The saying was published by Bloch (1980) in a humorous collection of "laws" in the manner of Murphy's, under the name of the reader who had sent it to him. It invites preferring explanations by error, negligence or ignorance over explanations by intent to harm.

Status: a rule of caution (heuristic), not a verified observation.

## Competing definitions and debates

The saying does not claim that malice does not exist: it requires that it be proven. Used in reverse, it can serve to excuse in advance a repeated or intentional fault. It aligns with Occam's razor, since an error generally requires fewer elements than a hidden plan.

## Why it helps to discern

It curbs the tendency to see a plot behind every malfunction and to attribute to others intentions one would not attribute to oneself. It does not exempt one from examining the facts when they show intent.
