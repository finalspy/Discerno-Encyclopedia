## Definition

Observed by Simon Newcomb in 1881 and then by Frank Benford in 1938, the law states that in many data sets (river lengths, populations, accounting amounts) the first digit is 1 in about 30% of cases and 9 in fewer than 5%. Hill (1995) gives a mathematical explanation.

Status: a mathematical regularity, valid only for certain data sets, generally those spanning several orders of magnitude.

## Competing definitions and debates

The law is used to flag anomalies, for example in accounting audits. But a departure from the law is not proof of fraud: many honest data sets do not follow it. Deckert, Myagkov and Ordeshook (2011) show that it is at best problematic for detecting electoral fraud, in either direction: neither conformity nor deviation allows a conclusion.

## Why it helps to discern

A statistical test cited as proof must be adapted to the data. Before accepting "these figures do not follow Benford's law, so they are fabricated", ask whether these figures were supposed to follow it.
