## How it works

The opinion of an authority (an expert, an institution, a well-known figure) is used to support a claim, implying that this opinion is enough to make it true.

Relying on an expert is not fallacious in itself. Walton (1997) proposes critical questions to judge such an argument: is the authority competent in this precise field? Is their opinion consistent with that of other experts? Is it based on evidence? Do they have no stake in the matter? The argument becomes fallacious when these conditions are not met.

## Why it is problematic

Competence in one field is carried over to another, or an authority is cited without anyone knowing what it relies on. The opinion replaces the argument.

## How to detect it

- A well-known figure is quoted on a subject outside their field.
- The authority is not named ("scientists say", "studies show") and no source is given.
- A single opinion is presented, with no mention of possible disagreement among specialists.
- The argument boils down to "because he is the one who says so".

## How to protect yourself

- Check the person's competence on this precise subject, and any stake they may have.
- Look for what supports the opinion (data, method, agreement of other specialists).
- Ask for the exact source of any study mentioned.
