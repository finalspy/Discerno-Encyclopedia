## How it works

Levitsky and Ziblatt (2018) identify, among the indicators of authoritarian behavior, the denial of the legitimacy of political opponents: they are described as enemies, threats to the nation or criminals, without evidence or trial. Linz (1978) distinguishes "loyal" opposition, which accepts the rules of the game, from "disloyal" opposition, which rejects them; delegitimization consists in placing all opposition in the second category.

The technique makes the defeat of the opponent appear as a duty and their rights as an obstacle.

## Why it is problematic

A democracy requires that adversaries recognize each other's right to exist and to govern if they win. When one camp denies that right to the other, the shared rules (elections, courts, press) cease to be respected.

## How to detect it

- The opponent is described as an enemy of the people or the nation, or as illegitimate by nature.
- Disagreement is presented as betrayal or moral fault, not as a position.
- Results or decisions that disadvantage the camp are declared fraudulent without evidence.

## How to protect yourself

- Apply the swap test: would the same speech by the opposing camp be acceptable?
- Distinguish criticism of an opponent's acts or proposals from the denial of their right to take part.
- Look for evidence behind any accusation of fraud or treason.

## How to respond

- Ask for evidence and for the legal avenues provided (appeals, verification, courts).
- Restate the shared rules calmly and with argument.
- Avoid responding with the same delegitimization, which validates the technique.
