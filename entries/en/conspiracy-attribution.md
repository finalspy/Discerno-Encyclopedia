## How it works

Douglas and colleagues (2019) describe conspiracy theories as explanations that attribute major events to powerful groups acting in secret. They meet needs for understanding, control and belonging. Popper (1945) criticized the "conspiracy theory of society": the idea that social phenomena are explained by the will of a few people who wanted them. Hofstadter (1964) described the "paranoid style": an alarmist tone, an all-powerful enemy and a certainty that is never corrected.

Real conspiracies exist and are established by evidence (documents, trials, investigations). What characterizes the process is not the idea of a conspiracy in itself, but the systematic attribution, without verifiable evidence and in an unfalsifiable way: the absence of evidence is taken as proof of concealment.

## Why it is problematic

It assigns blame without evidence, sustains general distrust of institutions and experts, and can be used to justify hatred of a group. It is hard to refute, since every objection is taken as proof of the conspiracy.

## How to detect it

- A vague, all-powerful group ("they", "the elite", "the powerful") is held responsible for varied events.
- Any element that contradicts the explanation is presented as part of the concealment.
- The explanation cannot be tested: no fact could refute it.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "What verifiable evidence supports this, and which evidence would refute it?"
- Ask whether simpler explanations (error, interest, chance) account for the facts.
- Distinguish a conspiracy established by evidence from an accusation that rests only on distrust.

## How to respond

- Ask questions rather than contradict: this keeps the dialogue open.
- Offer verifiable sources, and accept that a person may not change their mind right away.
- Avoid contempt, which strengthens the group membership of those who share the belief.
