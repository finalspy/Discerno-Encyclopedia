## How it works

Chapman (1967) presents pairs of words distributed in a balanced way. Participants then overestimate the frequency of certain pairs, particularly those whose words are associated in meaning.

Hamilton and Gifford (1976) describe two fictitious groups, one of which is smaller, with the same proportion of desirable and undesirable behaviors. Participants associate the undesirable behaviors, which are rarer, more strongly with the minority group: two infrequent elements stand out together and seem linked. The effect then rests on no real difference between the groups.

## Why it is problematic

An impression of a link can form without any data to justify it, then strengthen with each case that confirms it. It can fuel stereotypes about any group and make a remedy or method seem effective.

## How to detect it

- A link is asserted based on a few striking examples.
- Only the cases where the two elements are present together are cited.
- A sentence generalizes about a group without any comparison with the others.

## How to protect yourself

- Mentally fill in the four cells: A with B, A without B, B without A, neither A nor B.
- Look for a quantified comparison before concluding that a link exists.
- Even if a link exists, ask whether it indicates a cause, a common factor or a coincidence.
