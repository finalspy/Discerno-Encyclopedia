## How it works

Zajonc (1968) showed participants stimuli they did not know, such as meaningless words, characters resembling Chinese, or faces, more or less often. The more a stimulus had been presented, the more favorably it was judged afterward.

Bornstein's (1989) meta-analysis confirms the effect across many studies and indicates that it has limits: it does not grow without end with the number of exposures.

## Why it is problematic

Familiarity can be mistaken for quality. A name, a slogan or an idea can gain favor simply from being seen repeatedly, without any reason to like it being given. This effect concerns attitude, whereas the illusory truth effect concerns the belief that a statement is true.

## How to detect it

- A message keeps coming back in the same form, with no new argument.
- One has a favorable impression of a product, a person or an idea without being able to say why.
- The same formula is repeated by sources that seem different.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "What do I know about this, apart from having seen it often?"
- Compare with a less familiar option on criteria written in advance.
- Reduce your exposure to repetitive content before making an important decision.
