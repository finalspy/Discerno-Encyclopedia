## How it works

Whoever makes a claim must provide reasons for it. The reversal consists in presenting the claim as established until the other party has proven otherwise, or in concluding that it is true because no one has shown it to be false.

The burden of proof does not always lie on the same side: Walton (1988) and Hahn and Oaksford (2007) point out that it can shift depending on the context (debate, trial, decision to be made) and on the weight of the arguments already put forward. It does not shift merely because someone asserts something and the other party cannot refute it immediately.

Neutral example: "This dietary supplement improves concentration, prove me wrong."

## Why it is problematic

A claim without proof appears solid because the other party cannot refute it on the spot. The absence of contrary proof is not proof: this allows anything to be asserted at little cost, and wears the interlocutor down through the effort of refutation.

## How to detect it

- Phrases such as "prove that it is false", "no one has shown that…", "it has never been refuted".
- No evidence is given for the claim itself.
- The reply to a request for a source is to ask the other person for a source.

## How to protect yourself

- Ask: "What supports this claim?" before trying to refute it.
- Distinguish "not proven" from "proven false": the first situation allows no conclusion.
- Check whether the context really shifts the burden (for example, an institution making a decision) before accepting that it changes sides.
