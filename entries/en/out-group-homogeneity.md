## How it works

Quattrone and Jones (1980) show students at two universities the decision of a student from one of the two institutions. Participants are more willing to generalize to the whole group when the student is from the other university than from their own: a single case then seems more representative to them.

Park and Rothbart (1982) show that people recall more individual details about members of their own group than about members of other groups. We often think: "we are diverse, they are all alike."

## Why it is problematic

Attributing the acts of a few members to an entire group makes hasty generalizations possible. An isolated example can then be used to judge people who had no part in it. This bias can apply to any group, and everyone can be the "them" of someone else.

## How to detect it

- Phrases such as "they are all", "those people", "them, they want": an entire group becomes a single actor.
- An extreme case is presented as typical of the group.
- The author's own side is described with its nuances, the other side without any.

## How to protect yourself

- Look for the internal disagreements within the group described: they almost always exist.
- Ask what part of the group is actually concerned.
- Replace "they" with a precise description of who did what.
