## Definition

Diethelm and McKee (2009) describe denialism as the use of rhetorical tactics to give the appearance of a legitimate debate where there is none, in order to reject a solid scientific conclusion. They identify five tactics: relying on fake experts, invoking conspiracies, selecting what suits, setting impossible standards of proof, and distorting opposing arguments through fallacious reasoning.

Hansson (2017) sees a form of pseudoscience in it: denialism takes on the form of scientific debate without respecting its rules.

## Competing definitions and debates

The boundary with legitimate skepticism is debated. Challenging a conclusion with data and methods is the normal functioning of science. Denialism is recognized by its tactics (the five markers above), not by the fact of contesting a consensus.

The label is itself used abusively to discredit opponents on genuine disagreements, for example when the data are still uncertain or when the question concerns value choices more than facts.

Denialism is not tied to any camp. History offers cases of opposite natures: the minimization of tobacco risks by an industry, and the Lysenko affair, in which genetics was rejected in the Soviet Union in the name of a state doctrine (Joravsky, 1970).

## Why it helps to discern

Recognizing the tactic makes it possible to react to the type of argument rather than to the conclusion: the same five markers apply whatever the subject. A useful question: does this person accept evidence that would contradict their position, and which evidence?
